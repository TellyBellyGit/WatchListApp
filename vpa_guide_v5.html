1. Read the candle in context

Choose a candle, relative volume and where it appears. The interpretation changes with context: the same shape can mean different things at support, resistance or in the middle of a range.

Remember

Effort vs result

Volume is the effort. Spread, direction and closing position show the result. Ask whether that amount of activity produced a proportionate price move.

Avoid this shortcut

High volume ≠ buying

Every completed trade has both a buyer and a seller. Volume alone does not identify who initiated the trade, who is accumulating, or who is distributing.

2. Core VPA / VSA principles

These are interpretive frameworks, not mechanical buy/sell signals. Select a principle to see the observation, possible explanation, context and evidence that would change the reading.

Terminology note: Traditional No Supply generally means a narrow-spread down candle on low volume; traditional No Demand generally means a narrow-spread up candle on low volume. A low-volume, wide-spread surge is better described separately as a thin-liquidity move or “air pocket.”

3. Structure, liquidity and the order book

Candles tell you what traded prices did. They do not show the whole mechanism behind every move. Use each lens for what it can actually tell you.

Market structure

Where is price in the bigger picture?

  • Higher highs and higher lows: an advancing structure, while those swing points hold.
  • Support / resistance: areas where price previously turned or paused; not guaranteed walls.
  • Breakout: price moves beyond a meaningful boundary. A close beyond it is useful evidence, not a guarantee.
  • Retest: price revisits the boundary. Holding it supports the breakout thesis; losing it weakens that thesis.
  • Location matters: a bullish candle into nearby resistance has less room than the same candle breaking into open space.
Order book

Displayed bids, asks and spread

  • Bid: displayed buying interest; ask: displayed selling interest.
  • Spread: the gap between best bid and ask. A wide spread increases friction and uncertainty.
  • Depth: displayed size at price levels. A thin book can allow price to jump on relatively modest orders.
  • Market impact: an order may move price if nearby liquidity is limited.
  • Limit: displayed orders can be cancelled or changed; the book is not a promise that liquidity will remain.
Absorption

Heavy activity, little progress

If price repeatedly trades at a level with high volume but barely advances, aggressive orders may be meeting opposing liquidity. If the level holds and price turns, that is consistent with absorption.

But OHLCV alone cannot prove absorption, identify hidden orders or tell you which participant is responsible. Repeated executions and order-book/tape evidence can add detail.

False breakouts & “traps”

When a breakout cannot hold

  1. Price moves above a visible high or resistance.
  2. Buyers enter, but price fails to sustain the higher level.
  3. Price falls back below the boundary.
  4. A failed reclaim or renewed selling strengthens the failed-breakout reading.

Some breakout buyers may be trapped and sell as price weakens. But a failed breakout does not prove that a market maker deliberately engineered a trap.

Institutional activity

What can the chart really tell us?

Accumulation or distribution are possible explanations for certain sequences, not facts revealed by a candle. Look for repeated behaviour: effort, progress, level response, and follow-through across multiple bars.

Large volume can reflect institutions, retail activity, news, hedging or many participants trading with each other. Do not assign an identity without evidence.

Micro-cap caution

Thin liquidity changes the picture

A wide green candle on low volume can travel quickly because offers are sparse, not because broad demand is powerful. A wide red candle on low volume can fall because bids are thin.

Check spread, volume relative to recent bars, nearby levels and whether price holds when trading activity returns.

5. Quick reference: classic VPA / VSA ideas

A compact reminder of common concepts. The interpretation always depends on trend, location, preceding action and follow-through.

Harmony / validation

Wide spread and increased volume in the direction of movement can indicate that effort is producing a meaningful result.

Potential strength

Stronger at a valid breakout or in trend; weaker if immediately rejected.

Divergence / anomaly

High volume but little price progress suggests effort is meeting opposition or the market is pausing.

Investigate

Could be absorption, two-way trade or temporary balance; it is not automatically a reversal.

No Supply · traditional

A down candle with narrow spread and low volume, especially during an uptrend or pullback.

Potentially supportive

Suggests limited selling effort. A subsequent bullish response improves the case.

No Demand · traditional

An up candle with narrow spread and low volume, especially in a downtrend or near resistance.

Potential weakness

Suggests limited buying effort. It is more meaningful when the background is weak.

Stopping volume

Very high volume near the end of a decline, sometimes with a lower wick or reduced downward progress.

Possible selling climax

Potential buyers may be absorbing selling, but the low must hold and recovery needs follow-through.

Climactic buying / blow-off

Very high volume after an extended rise, especially with rejection or poor progress.

Exhaustion risk

Late demand may be meeting supply. Wait for weakness to confirm rather than assuming the top.

Upthrust

Price pushes above a prior high but fails and closes back down, often with a prominent upper wick.

Potential weakness

A failed breakout becomes more persuasive if price cannot reclaim the level.

Spring

Price briefly breaks below a trading range or support, then quickly recovers back into the range.

Potential strength

A sustained recovery is important. A clean breakdown that continues lower is not a spring.

Test / low-volume retest

Price revisits a prior low or breakout area on reduced volume and a narrow spread.

Possible lack of opposition

A successful test is followed by a positive response; low volume alone is not enough.

Churn

High volume but relatively limited price progress, sometimes after a substantial move.

Two-way activity

May reflect absorption or balance. Location and subsequent direction help distinguish possibilities.

Air pocket · practical term

A wide-spread move on low volume, up or down, where thin opposing liquidity may let price travel easily.

Liquidity-vulnerable

Do not confuse this with traditional No Supply or No Demand.

Follow-through

The response after an initial signal: continuation, hold, reclaim, rejection or failure.

Evidence check

Follow-through does not guarantee success; it helps test the initial interpretation.

Before treating a pattern as meaningful

  • What happened before this candle?
  • What did volume accomplish relative to the spread and close?
  • Is price at a meaningful level or in random mid-range space?
  • Does the wider trend and structure support the interpretation?
  • What price action would invalidate the idea?
  • Is there a separate entry trigger and a clearly defined risk?
Core mental model: Price shows the movement. Volume shows activity. VPA compares effort with result. Context tells you whether it matters. Price action provides the trigger.

7. Micro-cap and low-float cautions

Classic VSA was developed on deeper markets. On thin names the same bars can mean less, and a good "no" or "wait" is a valid answer.

Wide and quiet

A wide green bar on near-average volume may have moved through a few offers. Strength is unproven until price holds as activity returns.

Spread is part of the signal

A wide bid-ask spread raises the cost of being wrong and can make candle closes less meaningful. Check it before trusting a bar.

Volume arrives in bursts

Relative volume against the last few bars can mislead when one bar dominates. Compare against a longer average and the opening burst.

Rules that weaken

No Supply and No Demand need enough participation to mean anything. On very thin stocks a low-volume bar may simply be no one trading.
