Structural Stop-Loss Framework

A practical way to place stops using price structure rather than an arbitrary fixed number of cents.

The central principle
A stop should sit beyond the price level that invalidates the trade thesis — with enough room to avoid being triggered by normal market noise.

Structure determines the stop.
The stop determines the risk per share.
The risk per share determines the position size.

1Consolidation Base Floor

When a stock builds a tight range before breaking out, the bottom of that range represents an area where buyers have repeatedly defended price.

How it works: If your thesis is “this stock is breaking out of this consolidation,” the bottom of the consolidation is a logical structural invalidation point.

Placement: Your stop goes below the relevant structural floor, rather than simply below the last candle.

2Immediate Pullback / Higher-Low

This is particularly important for momentum trading. A stock does not have to fall all the way back to a major base before its immediate bullish structure is broken.

The most recent higher low can be the structural stop.

“I am buying because buyers have established a higher-low structure. If that higher low fails, my immediate bullish thesis is invalidated.”

3Major Swing / Pivot Low

This is the larger-timeframe structural stop.

The major pivot is farther away than the immediate pullback low. This gives the trade more room, but creates a larger risk-per-share.

You don't automatically choose the major pivot simply because it is stronger structure.

You ask: “Does the trade thesis require this much room?”

4Moving-Average / VWAP Structure

Your MEDS chart illustrates this particularly well. The green 9 EMA rises underneath price throughout the move.

Don't confuse a moving average with an automatic stop level.

A stock can temporarily pierce the 9 EMA and recover. Ask:

  • Did price close below it?
  • Was the move accompanied by expanding volume?
  • Did the stock lose its higher-low structure?
  • Did it lose VWAP as well?
  • Did selling pressure actually change the character of the move?
Structural stop vs. indicator stop: An EMA is an indicator. A pivot low is price structure. Price structure generally tells you more directly where the market has actually established support.

5Breakout Level / Former Resistance

A stock breaks through resistance. That old resistance can become new support.

If you buy the breakout, you can ask: “What price level would tell me that this breakout has failed?”

That level is more meaningful than simply saying: “I'll risk 10 cents.”

The Stop “Buffer”

Placing the stop exactly at the structural level can be problematic. A normal liquidity probe can briefly penetrate support and recover.

For example: Support = $5.00; Stop = $4.97. The exact buffer should not be an arbitrary universal number. It depends on volatility, spread, liquidity, stock price, candle size, ATR, time of day, and the particular setup.

Stop Placement Comes Before Position Size

WRONG
“I want 1,000 shares, therefore I'll put my stop $0.10 away.”
RIGHT
“The chart says my structural invalidation is $0.25 away. Therefore I calculate the number of shares I can afford.”
Share Size = Maximum Dollar Risk ÷ (Entry Price − Structural Stop Price)

Example

Account risk: $100
Entry: $5.00
Structural stop: $4.75

Risk/share = $5.00 − $4.75 = $0.25

Position size = $100 ÷ $0.25 = 400 shares

400 shares × $0.25 = $100 maximum planned risk.

Don't Move the Stop to Make the Position Fit

Suppose the chart gives you:

Entry = $5.00
Structural stop = $4.70

Your risk is $0.30/share.

$100 ÷ $0.30 = 333 shares

You should not say:

“333 shares isn't enough. I'll move the stop to $4.85 so I can buy 666 shares.”

That reverses the process.

Chart Structure→ Invalidation→ Stop→ Risk/Share→ Position Size→ Entry

“No Valid Structure = No Trade”

Sometimes you look at a chart and there simply isn't a sensible structural stop.

There may be no obvious nearby level that invalidates the trade. If the only logical stop is so far away that the position becomes too small — or the risk becomes unacceptable — the setup may simply not be suitable for the trade.

If you cannot identify a logical structural invalidation point, you do not have a properly defined trade.

The Three Scales of Structure

Your MEDS chart makes this especially clear. A single chart can contain micro, intermediate and major structure simultaneously.

The stop is not chosen independently of the setup. It is chosen because of the setup.

The Complete Framework

The best structural anchor is the one that most directly invalidates the reason for entering the trade.

Position Size = Maximum Dollar Risk ÷ (Entry Price − Structural Stop Price)
Structure→ Stop→ Risk / Share→ Position Size→ Entry

Do not start with the number of shares you want to buy.
Start with the chart. Identify what proves the trade thesis wrong. Place the stop beyond that structure. Then calculate the position size.
